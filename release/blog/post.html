How far is your nearest bus, and when does the last one leave? I counted every timetabled bus departure in England on an ordinary Tuesday, Saturday and Sunday and checked every village. 41% have no stop with four or more weekday buses within 800 metres, and 68% have no Sunday bus within that distance.

Most of what we hear about rural buses is about loss. Bus mileage in England outside London is down by more than a quarter since 2005, according to the Department for Transport's own statistics. Campaigners have counted thousands of routes reduced or withdrawn. What is harder to find is a simple picture of what is left: which villages still have a bus, how often it comes, and whether you can get home on it in the evening.

That picture is now possible to draw. Every bus operator in England has to publish its timetables as open data through the Department for Transport's Bus Open Data Service, and the department publishes them as one file per region. I took all nine regional files, counted the buses leaving every one of 251,790 bus stops on a Tuesday, a Saturday and a Sunday in 2026, and then asked the same questions of every village in Wikidata.

A third of villages have no weekday bus nearby

I checked 15,012 English villages. For 4,891 of them, 33%, there was no stop within 800 metres with a single bus on the Tuesday. That is about a ten minute walk, as the crow flies. For 2,966 villages the nearest weekday bus was more than 1.6 km away.

Another 1,318 villages had a stop with only one, two or three buses all day. Sometimes that is a school run; sometimes it is a bus into town in the morning and one back in the afternoon. It is rarely enough to get to work. Put those together and 6,209 villages, 41%, have no usable weekday bus. For hamlets it is 60%. Towns, by contrast, are almost all served: only 7 of 1,158 are not.

The last bus home

Having a bus is one thing. Getting home on it is another. Of the villages with any weekday bus within 800 metres, the last one leaves before seven in the evening in 4,342 of 10,119. Across all villages, 62% have no bus from 19:00, and 68% have none on a Sunday. Even among villages with a usable weekday service, a third have nothing in the evening and almost half have nothing on Sunday. Saturday, interestingly, is nearly as good as a weekday.

Only 30% of villages have all three: a usable weekday service, an evening bus and a Sunday bus. On the map there is a clock you can drag through the evening. At six o'clock most of the network is still there. By nine, the countryside has mostly gone grey.

Who lives in a bus desert

Villages are places, not people, so I also looked at England's 33,755 small statistical areas, which hold about 1,700 people each. 6.0 million people, 10.5% of the population, live in one with no usable weekday bus within 800 metres of its centre point. In rural areas that is 46% of residents; in towns and cities, 3.5%. In the smallest and most remote rural areas it is 70%, and 88% have no Sunday bus.

{{IMG|b_rural.png|Bars by type of area: residents with no usable weekday bus rise from 3% in urban areas near a city to 70% in smaller rural areas far from one, with even higher shares for no evening bus and no Sunday bus|Small areas by the official 2021 rural and urban classes.}}

Before I looked at any of this, I wrote down what I expected. I thought rural areas would be worse served. They are, by a long way. I thought that once you compare like with like, more deprived areas would be better served, because buses tend to follow the people who use them, and those are often people without a car. They are, though inside the countryside the pattern is a hump: the most deprived and the least deprived rural areas both do better than the ones in the middle. And I thought areas with more older people would be worse served. They are too: 2.0 million people aged 60 and over live in small areas with no usable weekday bus.

{{IMG|b_deprivation.png|Line chart by deprivation decile: in rural areas the share with no usable bus is low in the most deprived decile, over half in the middle deciles and lower again in the least deprived; in urban areas it stays low throughout|Inside the countryside, a hump rather than a slope.}}

One check did not come out the way it should have. I included a test that ought to show nothing: a measure of housing quality (homes in poor condition or with poor energy performance), which has no obvious reason to be linked to buses. It was linked. That tells me the official rural and urban classes do not capture every difference between places, so the patterns for deprivation and age are worth knowing about, but they are descriptions, not causes.

Busy cities, empty lanes

Counting each bus once at every stop it calls at, England has 260 weekday stop departures for every 1,000 residents, and London 473. Outside London the range runs from 425 in the West Midlands to 146 in the East of England. But the West Midlands also has the highest share of villages with no usable weekday bus, 50%. A region can have a busy city network and a very thin rural one at the same time, and a regional average hides that.

How I did this

The timetables come from the Bus Open Data Service. I counted the departures at every stop on one Tuesday, Saturday and Sunday in 2026, kept bus and coach stops, and removed stops that appear in two regional files. For each village I found the nearest stop with a weekday bus, and among the stops within 800 metres, the busiest one, the last departure, and whether anything runs after seven in the evening or on a Sunday. I read every town that came out as a bus desert by eye, which turned up a few Wikidata points in the wrong place and two new towns that were never built. The small areas use the English Indices of Deprivation 2025 and the official rural and urban classes. I wrote the full analysis plan before computing any result, and the paper reports every check in it.

There are real limits. It is one week's timetables. School buses, dial a ride services and community transport may be missing, and some councils rely on exactly those in the places that show up here as deserts. A few places, such as Hawes in the Yorkshire Dales, have stops in the files but no Tuesday trips, which looks like a gap in the data. Distances are straight lines, not walks along lanes without pavements, and each small area is placed at a point inside it, not where most of its people live. And a bus calling at a stop is not the same as a bus going somewhere you need to go.

Because the timetables are published all the time, the same code can be run again next year to see whether these numbers go up or down. If you live in a village, the map will tell you what the timetables say about yours.
